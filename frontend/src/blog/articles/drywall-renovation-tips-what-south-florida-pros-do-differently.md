# Drywall During a South Florida Renovation: What to Decide First

Drywall is usually discussed near the end of a renovation, when everyone is thinking about paint. The decisions that determine the result come earlier: whether a wall is dry and sound, what work must be inspected behind it, and how smooth the finished surface needs to be. If those questions are answered after the boards go up, correcting them means opening finished work.

## First decide whether the wall is ready to close

Before replacing board, identify why the old surface failed. A stain can come from a roof leak, plumbing, air-conditioning condensation, or water entering at an opening. Covering the stain without finding the source leaves the problem behind the new finish. The [EPA's moisture guidance](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) puts moisture control at the center of mold prevention; damaged materials and hidden cavities need assessment and drying before enclosure.

Ask what will be checked while the wall is open: the substrate, any visible moisture damage, insulation, and the plumbing or electrical work in the cavity. If a permitted trade or structural change is part of the project, ask the local building department or contractor which inspection must happen before closure. Permit requirements differ by scope and jurisdiction. Fort Lauderdale's [current guidance](https://www.fortlauderdale.gov/Government/Departments/Development-Services/HB-803) distinguishes possible minor interior work from structural and utility changes; it does not make every drywall job permit-free.

## Specify the finish you expect to see

“Ready for paint” is not a precise finish specification. The [Gypsum Association's GA-214 guidance](https://gypsum.org/2022/02/revised-levels-of-finish-for-gypsum-panel-products-released/) describes levels of finish so designers and contractors can choose an appearance appropriate to the final decoration and lighting. Ask which level is included in the proposal, and view a sample where possible. Bright side light, dark paint, and glossy finishes reveal surface variation more readily than a utility-room finish.

A useful scope names the rooms, finish level, corner treatment, texture if any, primer, and how existing surfaces will meet new ones. If only one part of a room is repaired, ask whether the price includes blending the patch across the visible plane. A perfectly finished patch can still stand out when the surrounding wall has a different texture.

## Plan for dust, moisture, and people living in the home

Sanding and demolition affect adjacent rooms. Ask how the work area will be separated, how supply and return vents will be protected, where debris will go, and who cleans before primer. Ask how indoor moisture will be controlled while compound dries and before paint is applied. A schedule should allow the materials to dry under the actual site conditions rather than promising a fixed number of days for every house.

If a bathroom or other wet area is involved, ask the designer or contractor which board, backing, waterproofing system, and finish belong in each location. Board color alone is not a waterproofing plan. Request the product and installation details for the assembly, especially around tubs, showers, and penetrations.

## Choose a small repair or a larger renovation scope

A sound, dry wall with a small isolated hole may only need a local patch. Repeated staining, soft material, a wall that must be opened for trades, or a room-wide finish change calls for a broader assessment. This distinction protects you from paying for a beautiful surface over an unresolved cause.
