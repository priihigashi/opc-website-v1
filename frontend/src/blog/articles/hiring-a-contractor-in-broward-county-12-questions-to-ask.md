# Hiring a Contractor in Broward County: 12 Questions to Ask

Hiring a contractor here is not the same as hiring one anywhere else. Broward sits inside a
hurricane-zone code that most of the country never deals with, permitting runs through your individual
city rather than one county office, and flood zone can quietly reshape a project before it starts.

Most homeowners know they should ask questions. Fewer know which ones matter *here*. These twelve do.

## Licence, insurance, and whether they can legally do the work

### 1. Does your Florida license cover this work?

Ask for the license number, type and business name, then check the current record yourself through
Florida [DBPR's license search](https://www.myfloridalicense.com/wl11.asp). Make sure the license
class fits the proposed work and the proposal names the business you are hiring. Oak Park Construction
is listed under Matthew McFolling's active **Certified Building Contractor** license, CBC1263425;
[DBPR's record](https://www.myfloridalicense.com/LicenseDetail.asp?SID=&id=BD9747F01F094DD9A193F8D24BF3A91B)
shows the Oak Park Construction DBA. Check it again before signing because status can change.

If a bidder will not give you a license number or the record does not match, pause the hiring decision
and resolve that discrepancy before paying a deposit.

### 2. Do you carry general liability insurance and workers' compensation?

Ask for a certificate of insurance, not a verbal yes. Check that the policy is current and that the
coverage is sized for your project.

Then ask the follow-up most people miss: **do your subcontractors carry their own?** Most contractors
use subs. Ask your insurer or adviser what coverage and documentation are appropriate for your project; do not infer coverage from a verbal answer.

### 3. Will you pull all the required permits yourself?

Permitting in Broward runs through individual cities, not one county office. Pompano Beach, Fort
Lauderdale and Hollywood each have their own submittal requirements and their own review times. Your
contractor should know your city's process and handle it start to finish.

Ask the building department which permits the exact scope requires and who will apply for them. If a
bidder proposes owner-pulled permits, ask the department what responsibilities that creates before
accepting the arrangement. Keep permit and final-inspection records with your house documents.

## Scope, timeline and money

### 4. What exactly is in this contract, and what isn't?

This is where homeowners get hurt most often. A low bid means nothing if the scope is vague.

Have them walk you through every line. Is demolition included? Debris removal? Permit fees?
Inspections? Final cleanup? Then look hard at **allowances** — the placeholder amounts for tile,
fixtures and cabinetry. Allowances are where a reasonable-looking contract quietly becomes an expensive
one. Ask what the allowance actually buys, and ask to see something at that price.

### 5. What is a realistic timeline, and what could move it?

Weather, material lead times and permit review can move exterior work. Ask which dates in the proposed
schedule depend on approvals, deliveries or inspections.

A good contractor gives you a range and names the risks. Ask what has actually delayed their recent
projects. That answer tells you more about their experience than anything on their website.

### 6. How do you handle change orders?

Something unexpected turns up on nearly every project that touches an existing building. What matters
is what happens next.

Ask directly: *will I get a written change order, with a price and a schedule impact, before any extra
work starts?* If the answer is anything other than yes, keep looking.

### 7. What is the payment schedule?

Payments should track progress — deposit, then milestones like foundation complete, framing inspected,
rough-ins done, final walkthrough. That structure protects both sides.

A reasonable deposit is normal. A large lump sum before anyone mobilises is not.

## Structure, code, and the things specific to South Florida

### 8. How does the Florida Building Code affect this specific project?

Broward is in the Florida Building Code's High Velocity Hurricane Zone. The project designer and
local building department determine the applicable wind requirements, opening protection and structural
connections for the specific work. Ask how those requirements appear in the drawings and bid.

Ask them to explain, in plain language, how that shapes *your* project. A contractor who has built here
can do that without notes. One who cannot has probably not built here long.

### 9. Is this property in a flood zone, and what does that change?

Flood information can affect the design and approvals for certain work. Search the address first, then
ask the designer and building department whether elevation information or other flood-related
requirements apply to your proposal.

Ask whether you need a survey or an elevation certificate before design starts — not after. Broward
publishes [flood information by property](https://www.broward.org/ENVIRONMENT),
and FEMA's [flood map service centre](https://msc.fema.gov/portal/search) shows the mapped zone.

### 10. Who is actually on site every day?

Ask who runs your job day to day, how often they are there, and who you call when something is wrong.
"The owner will be involved" is not an answer. A name is.

### 11. Can I see work you finished, and talk to those owners?

Photos prove a job existed. A conversation proves how it went. Ask for owners from projects of a
similar type and size, and ask them one specific question: *what happened when something went wrong?*

### 12. What does the end look like?

Ask how a project closes: the punch list, the final inspection, what warranty covers, for how long, and
who to call in eighteen months. A contractor who has thought about the end has usually thought about
the middle too.

## What good answers sound like

You are not testing anyone's memory. You are listening for whether they have done this here, recently,
and whether they will put things in writing.

Vagueness on scope, reluctance about permits, pressure for a large deposit, or hand-waving about
hurricane code — any of those is worth more attention than the price on the last page.
